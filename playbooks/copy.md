# Copy

How to write, test and QA cold email copy: the frameworks, the lines, the sequence and the checks before anything ships.

| Used by | Copywriter |
|---|---|
| Read with | [taste.md](taste.md), [opinions.md](opinions.md), [offers.md](offers.md), [market-research.md](market-research.md), [lists.md](lists.md), [infrastructure.md](infrastructure.md), [inbox.md](inbox.md) |

These are GrowthFlare's defaults. Follow them unless your own campaign notes override one on purpose. If a rule here conflicts with a task, stop and ask the human who owns the campaign. Do not quietly pick one.

## The core idea

- The offer is everything. If the offer is right, the copy rarely matters. Find the winning offer first, then tune the rest. Offer building lives in [offers.md](offers.md).
- Lead with the outcome, not the problem. Open on what they get. Their problems are assumed. They already know them.
- Short beats long in about 90% of cases. Trust and patience both start low, and a long email makes them work before they get anything.
- Short also helps deliverability. Inbox providers read short, plain emails as more likely to be human and internal, so they land in the primary inbox more often.
- Voice: every email reads like a text from a friend. Industry does not change this. There is no "professional" floor for serious markets.
- Make it about them. Keep at least 3 sentences about the recipient for every 1 about you or your client. Never open with "We are X, we do Y".
- We are students of the market. Every campaign is a way to learn what the market responds to. Do not guess. Test.

## Before you write: intake

Do not write until you have items 1 to 3. Push hard for 4 and 5.

1. How many angles to deploy.
2. The offers to test (up to 8). For each: what the prospect receives, the format, and how long it takes them.
3. The persona: role, industry, company size.
4. Real customer language: sales call transcripts, onboarding notes, objections, pains. Real data beats invented pains. See [market-research.md](market-research.md).
5. Your own past campaigns. Look at your own past campaigns in your sending platform for what worked: subject lines, word counts, offers, CTAs, and which ones flopped.

Also confirm the testing phase: Phase 1 (no winning offer yet) or Phase 2 (winner found, now testing framing).

## The four-step loop

Get approval at each step before the next: (1) direction: phase, vertical, offers in scope, priority order, and what your past campaigns say worked or failed; (2) the offer line approach and subject lines per offer; (3) the deployment table (format below); (4) the final copy in priority order, with follow-ups and timing.

## Framework A: the default

Lean on it heavily, especially early in a campaign. The shape is fixed. Everything inside it flexes.

```
{first_name}, I [built / put together] [valuable thing] that helps [type of person]
[reach result] without [pain point / objection].
[soft ask]

[Signature]
P.S. [optional: proof, or a risk reversal tied to a known objection]
```

- **The engine is the value prop:** [thing] that gets [result] without [pain]. The "without" clause names the problem as something they avoid, not a wound you reopen.
- **The valuable thing swaps by offer:** a free slice of the service, a Loom, a lead magnet, a teardown, an audit, a document. Lead magnet example: "a breakdown of the last 1.5M cold emails we sent that helps accountants book meetings without scaling their SDR team".
- **The ask swaps too.** Pick from the CTA library below.
- **Write the result as its own verb.** "Get" is on the banned word list, so write "helps accountants book meetings", not "helps accountants get meetings".
- **No separate opener.** The value line is the email. No warm-up line and no personalized first line in front of it.
- **Target length:** 25 to 35 words, 2 to 3 sentences. When the offer is strong enough, one sentence is enough.
- **Needs more room?** Use two short numbered points, then the ask. Too complex even for that? Do not write a longer email. Offer a sales asset (a document, an overview, a deck) and let it explain.

### The work-asymmetry variation

Worth testing, best for free deliverable offers. It is a variation, not the default frame.

```
{first_name}, if we could [specific free deliverable] completely free
(we'd [3 or 4 things you handle], you just [one small action]),
would that be worth hearing more?
```

It makes the value-to-effort ratio obvious at a glance. Write the parenthetical with a comma or a second sentence. Never a dash.

Generic example: "{first_name}, if we could edit your next podcast episode completely free (we'd cut it, write the show notes and design the cover, you just record), would that be worth hearing more?"

## Framework B: sparing secondary

The question form of poke-the-bear. Allowed as a secondary test. Never the lead, never the opener of a campaign.

```
{first_name}, [what if | imagine if | how differently would things look if] [positive outcome]?
[Happy to send | I can send | Want me to send] you a [specific free deliverable],
[personalized to you | with your name on it], to show how it could work?
[Signature]
```

- Opens with implied pain as a hypothetical, then moves straight to the offer.
- 35 to 45 words, 3 to 4 sentences. One question CTA.
- Best for broad lists with no useful per-prospect detail.
- Frameworks C and D (touchpoint, three ideas) are retired. Do not write them.

## Offer lines

The offer line is the email. If the reader has to work to find the value, it will not work.

- **Name four things:** the thing, who it is for, the result, and what they avoid.
- **Make the thing specific.** Not "a quick mock-up". Say a mock-up showing how companies like theirs reach a specific result at a specific rate. Specific to their industry reads as a little exclusive.
- **Leave some curiosity.** Say enough to seem unique and worth asking about. Not everything.
- **No made-up mechanism names.** Nothing like "triple backflip outbound system". If it sounds like AI wrote it, it does not ship.
- **One line, one value prop, one person.** With one variable, a reply or silence tells you whether that person cares about that value prop. Two value props in one line and you cannot tell which one got the reply.
- **Guarantees ride along, they are not the offer.** Put them in parentheses or the P.S.

## Subject lines

- 2 to 4 words, lowercase, no punctuation, exactly one merge token, about 16 characters at the median winner.
- Default: `fyi {first_name}`.
- Plain and internal-looking beats clever and curious. Do not tease the outcome in a salesy way.
- No "Re:" on a first email. The subject must work on its own.
- If you spin subject variants, every variant follows these rules.

## CTAs

One CTA per email, asked as one question. Never two. Low commitment, answerable in one word, 5 to 8 words. It should read like a text to a friend, not a meeting request.

No hard meeting ask and no calendar link in email 1, unless a strong signal (like hiring) means they already know they have the problem.

### The CTA library

| Offer | CTA |
|---|---|
| Default | would that be worth sharing more? |
| A video | worth sending a quick video explaining more? / worth a quick 2-min Loom explaining more? / can I send you over a quick video? |
| An asset or lead magnet | want me to send it over? / helpful if I sent over the playbook? / want the 1-pager, no pitch, just the framework? |
| A free deliverable | worth hearing more? |
| A teardown or method | should I send the teardown for {process}? / want me to send how I did it? |
| Anything | worth a quick look? / make sense to explore this? / interested? |

Why it stays soft: when the last thing they read is about a discussion, many decide they do not want to talk to you, even when interested. "Worth sharing more?" gets a yes from the people in the middle who are interested but do not have 10 minutes.

- **Be specific about what they get.** "Sharing more" is soft but vague. "A two minute video explaining more" names the thing and points them at something credible.
- **Match the CTA to awareness.** Least aware prospects respond to a low-intent ask (a Loom, a document). More aware prospects respond to a harder ask (book a call) or a free deliverable. Testing CTAs across that range is how you read the market's awareness.
- **Test 2 to 3 CTAs per offer** on a first launch. At minimum, one direct "call" version and one lead magnet version.
- **Judge CTAs on meetings booked, not replies.** A direct CTA might get 5 interested replies and 1 meeting. A lead magnet CTA might get 50 replies with 30% booking. The lead magnet wins.
- **A yes is not the finish line.** Reply with a specific time. Handling replies lives in [inbox.md](inbox.md).

## P.S. lines

Optional. When used, a P.S. carries one of two things, nothing else:

1. **Proof.** For example, a short list of relevant results under the signature. On one campaign, short copy only started working once a P.S. listed about 15 relevant case studies.
2. **A risk reversal aimed at a real objection.** Think about the prospect's first thought when they picture using your thing, and answer that. Not a generic money-back line. Structure example: "P.S. this would be done entirely on a performance basis, so you only pay for [the outcome you deliver]."

- Use guarantees more in less sophisticated markets (local service businesses). In sophisticated markets (e-commerce) a guarantee will not move anyone on its own.
- At low awareness the P.S. matters much less. You are only getting them to raise a hand.
- Test it as a variable: one version with the P.S., one without. Validate the core line first, then stack P.S. tests on top.
- Drop the P.S. from the follow-up.

## Follow-ups: only two moves

A follow-up does one of exactly two things:
1. **Cut words so the value is more visible.** Strip everything that is not the offer.
2. **Soften the CTA so the friction is lower.** If email 1 asked for a chat, email 2 asks "do you mind if I send a video explaining how that works?"

That is it. No new pitch, no agitating, no nagging.

- **Every follow-up stands alone.** A prospect who never saw email 1 must understand email 2 without scrolling up. No "as I mentioned", no references to earlier emails.
- **Banned openers:** "just following up", "just checking in", "circling back", "bumping this to the top", "one more thought".

## Sequence structure

Email 2 pulls about 50% of email 1's reply rate. Email 3 drops another 50%. Keep sequences short.

| Email | Send | Thread | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Day 0 | New thread | The full value line, soft ask, optional P.S. |
| 2 | Day 3 to 4 | Reply in thread | One of the two moves: fewer words or a softer CTA. No P.S. |
| 3 (optional) | Day 7 to 8 | New thread | The other move. Must stand alone with full context. |

## Angles in copy

### ICP angle (the introduction)

Offer to introduce the prospect to their own ideal customers, looking for the service they sell. Shape: `{first_name}, if we could introduce you to {ICP} looking for {their service}, [soft ask]`

- Why it works: they feel the work is already done. It reads as an intro, not a pitch. It even lands on a lead that slipped onto the wrong list.
- Write your own wording. If everyone sends the same line, it stops working.
- Expected results: one broad agency list with both variables got a 2.5% reply rate, 15% of those positive.
- Weakness: great at interest, weaker at booked calls that show, because intent is low.
- Fix the show rate: add a second line that sets a timeline ("a few of them over the next two to four weeks"), and a performance-basis P.S. if your pricing allows it.

### Loom angle

A short video as the low-friction offer. It appears in three places: the offer in email 1, the softer swap in email 2, or the answer when someone asks for more info.

- The email sells the Loom, the Loom sells the call, the call sells the service. The email names the interesting thing and asks permission to send it.
- Structure example: "I found a way to spot [type of business] that are spending on [thing] this quarter. Would it be useful if I sent that over?"
- Use it for less aware prospects, when a client will not give work away free, or when a campaign is getting nothing.
- Know your market. Some prefer a one-page doc and a call. In an unfamiliar market, test the Loom against a doc.
- Most people who ask for the Loom will not watch it. Plan for it: call interested leads first, then send the video. See [inbox.md](inbox.md).

### Poke-the-bear, and why it is rarely right

Poke-the-bear opens by telling them they have a problem: "most [role]s I talk to struggle with [thing]".

- If your copy has to remind them of their problem, the offer is weak. When replies are flat, fix the offer. Do not agitate harder.
- It costs you test data. Some readers think "I don't struggle with that", and you cannot tell if they rejected the offer or your assumption.
- Name the pain without poking: put it in the "without" clause, or lead with the thing that solves it. For example, after an ad platform change that means brands need far more creatives, do not say they are struggling. Say you built a way to produce the creatives they now need, fast, without filming.
- You have about 60 words. That is not enough to teach someone why your thing matters. Pick a stronger, broader offer instead.
- Never insult the prospect or the way they work now.
- Framework B is the only allowed form, as a sparing secondary test.

## Personalization rules

- **A good offer beats personalization every time.** Personalization only earns its place when it makes the offer more relevant to that person. Otherwise it is fluff.
- **No personalized first lines.** "Noticed your LinkedIn post about X" is not a reason to reach out. It is mostly a crutch for lazy list building.
- **Put the personalization inside the offer line.** "If we could introduce you to {ICP} looking for {their service}". The token makes the same offer land as if written for that slice of the list.
- **Personalization replaces segmentation you did not do.** A perfectly segmented list would not need it. It is a scaling tool, not a flourish.
- **Signals qualify the list, they stay out of the copy.** Hiring, funding and similar triggers tell you who belongs on the list. Do not announce them in the email. See [lists.md](lists.md).
- **Identity mirroring:** describe them with the label they would use about themselves, not a database job title.
- **Infer the worldview** from role, industry and size (lean team, founder-led, established company). Tie the offer to what they already invest in. State what you inferred in the rationale.

### AI-written variables: lock the prompt before you scale

- Run one lead, then batches of 10. Review each batch in a table, fix the prompt, rerun.
- Stop when 2 rounds in a row need zero corrections, or the owner says "lock it". A later correction resets the count. Then scale in batches of 10 to 20.
- Give each variable a definition, one good and one bad example, and hard rules: no invented facts, no dashes, no banned phrases, a max length.
- After scaling, spot-check 5 random leads per variant for repeated phrasing, invented facts, hedging and values that would break the merge.

## Spintax rules

- Spin the words, not the psychology. Every variant lands the same emotional note.
- Every combination must pass every rule here, and the spam check. One new option can multiply into hundreds of sendable combinations, so re-check after any spintax edit.
- If a spam hit shows up in every combination, it is in the fixed text, so fix the base sentence. If it shows up in some, fix only that option.

## Testing: offer first, then framing

### Phase 1: offer isolation

- One script per offer. Copy stays neutral and identical across tests, with no pain-point angles. The only thing that changes is the offer.
- Compare offers only across variants that share the same CTA, so the offer is the only difference. CTA variants are their own cells in the launch matrix.
- Winner: 8%+ positive reply rate. 15%+ is a winner at scale.

### Phase 2: framing isolation

- Lock the winning offer wording. One script per pain-point angle or persona segment. Change only the framing, hook or worldview.
- 45 to 60 words is fine here. Subject lines may shift to reflect the angle.

Never test the offer and the framing at the same time. Never start Phase 2 without a Phase 1 winner. On a first launch, run the test matrix (industry, persona, value prop, offer, CTA, 2 to 3 at each layer) from [opinions.md](opinions.md), still changing one thing per test.

### When to read results

| Sends per variant | Action |
|---|---|
| Under 500 | Too early. Ignore. |
| 500 | First directional read. Flag obvious winners and losers. |
| 1,000 | Readable. Confirm winners, kill losers, move volume. |
| 2,000+ | High confidence. Lock the winner, start Phase 2. |

- Kill candidate: under 2% positive reply rate. A variant winning by 2%+ is a meaningful difference.
- Do not scale volume until a campaign shows traction (3%+ reply rate).
- Never fall in love with an offer. Test offers you hate. The market decides.

### If nothing is winning

- Test an intro offer (something from your sales process that is valuable on its own), or a custom Loom about something interesting you noticed.
- Strip to bare bones: "[relevant thing]. If I could [outcome], would that be worth a conversation?"
- Try humor. It is an outlier offer creator. Or borrow the structure of your best past campaign from any vertical.

## QA scoring

Score every email before you return it. 1 point per check.

| Check | Pass |
|---|---|
| Opens on the value line. No personalized first line, no warm-up | 1 |
| No invented or assumed facts about the prospect | 1 |
| Variables are valid and named consistently for your sending platform | 1 |
| No banned words or phrases (spam check passed) | 1 |
| 3:1 recipient-to-sender ratio holds | 1 |
| Under about 50 words (Phase 2 up to 60) | 1 |
| One CTA, one binary question, 5 to 8 words | 1 |
| Reads naturally out loud, like a text from a friend | 1 |
| No em dashes or en dashes | 1 |
| Subject line follows the rules and works on its own | 1 |
| Offer names the thing, who it is for, the result, and what they avoid | 1 |
| Every email stands alone, no references to earlier emails | 1 |

**10 to 12:** ship it. **7 to 9:** revise first. **Under 7:** restart the script. Then run the reject-on-sight test and the finished checklist in [taste.md](taste.md).

## Spam check

Run the spam check as code. Never judge spam words by eye. Use the private skill repo: **github.com/growthflare-ignite/spam-check**. It holds the full banned list and expands every spintax combination.

Check two things every time: the copy, and the lead values that merge into it. Variables merge at send time, after anyone has read the copy. On one campaign to healthcare practices, the copy mostly passed, but 607 of 5,700 leads carried "Medical", "Home" and "Urgent" in through company name and company type. Run it on new copy, on any live or draft campaign, on every lead list before upload, and after every spintax edit.

### What the banned list covers

| Category | Common examples |
|---|---|
| Single words | get, new, now, open, compare, problem, deal, access, sales, marketing, cash, extra, invoice, purchase, credit, finance, insurance, medical, home, life, soon, urgent |
| Short phrases | following up, circle back, bumping this up, great fit, compare notes, one time, all good, off chance |
| Promotional or pressure | free trial, for free, free consultation, act now, limited time, click here, special offer, guaranteed results, no obligation, risk-free, increase sales, today, quote, discount, amazing |
| Phishing or security | verify identity, account update, password reset, final notice, confirm your details |
| Irrelevant categories | gambling, pharmacy, weight loss and miracle-cure language |

Token rules: punctuation, hyphens or joining words do not make a banned root safe. "cash-cycle" is still "cash". Close variants like "getting" count. Acronyms are a judgment call you must be able to defend.

**Hard fails:** banned words, em dashes, multiple exclamation marks, a greeting before the name (Hi, Hello, Hey), the sender in the third person ("[Company] helps"), dollar signs, fake urgency. **Review items, default to rewriting:** close variants of banned words, ALL CAPS, en dashes, multiple links.

### How to fix

- Keep the structure and spintax. Change only the flagged word or option.
- Swap pressure for permission. "click here" becomes "let me know and I can send it over". "free consultation" becomes "worth a short conversation".
- Company names with a banned word: remove the token if the name still reads clearly ("Access Brand Communications" becomes "AB Communications"). Some need a person to pick.
- Category variables ("home care services") need an agreed replacement, not just a deleted word.
- Do not defend borderline wording. Rewrite it. Never call copy fixed without a clean re-run.
- A clean check is not proof the copy will land. Long copy, links and sales tone still hurt. See [infrastructure.md](infrastructure.md).
