# Offers

How to build the cold email offer: the frameworks, the tests, the angles, and the 3 value props plus 3 front-end offers you hand to the copywriter.

## Start here: which "offer" are we talking about?

"Offer" means two different things. Don't mix them up.

- **The cold email offer** is the front-end thing you put in a cold email to earn a reply. This playbook is about this one.
- **The business offer** is the packaged thing the client sells on the sales call and in their marketing. Different layer, different rules.

Much of what operators teach (guarantees as the core lever, pricing, revenue walls) is written at the business-offer layer. If something here seems to contradict a framework you've read, check the layer first.

You build offers. You do not write email copy. That is [copy.md](copy.md). You do not set the client's pricing either.

## Offer vs service vs mechanism

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Offer | What the prospect **gets**: a specific pain solved, a specific outcome promised | "1,000,000 views in 90 days" |
| Service | What you **do** | "Short form video editing" |
| Mechanism | **How** you do it differently | A named process, or just a genuinely unique way of doing it |

Cold traffic buys offers. It never buys services.

- Wrong: "We provide staffing for biotech companies."
- Right: "Fill your open clinical roles in 30 days with pre-vetted candidates."

### Fazio's hierarchy (most to least important)

1. **Specific problem.** If it's big enough, nothing else matters.
2. **Unique mechanism.** How you solve it differently.
3. **Pricing or terms.**
4. **Risk reversal.**

If the one above is strong enough, the ones below barely matter.

Template: *Most {target audience} have {problem}. We help them get {desired end result} with {unique mechanism}.*

### Make the result concrete

- **Pick a dimension and put a number on it.** Views, leads, booked calls, new clients. This is the most battle-tested form of an offer.
- **Run the "so that" ladder.** Feature, so that, so that, until you reach revenue or profit. Push the implication as far as it goes.
- **Niche it down.** "We help businesses" is level 1. "US-based B2B SaaS companies with 10-50 employees stuck below their next revenue milestone" is level 5.
- **Pick a timeline.** Under 30 days: high urgency, lower credibility. 30-90 days: balanced. 90+ days: more credible, less urgent.

## The strong-offer test

An offer is strong when all three are true:

1. **It solves a pain validated from the prospect.** Not an assumed pain. Real, and usually revenue-linked. Comes from your research brief ([market-research.md](market-research.md)).
2. **It's easy to digest and act on.** Little friction, little commitment on the front end.
3. **It delivers the most value up front for the least work from the prospect.** That's the whole game.

And it has to be something they care about. A frictionless offer for a pain nobody feels is still dead.

### The value equation (Alex Hormozi)

**Value = (Dream Outcome x Perceived Likelihood) / (Time Delay x Effort and Sacrifice)**

| Lever | Push it | In cold email terms |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | Up | The claim. Big and specific |
| Perceived likelihood | Up | Proof, case studies, a guarantee |
| Time delay | Down | How fast they get it |
| Effort and sacrifice | Down | How little they have to do |

The strongest offer scores well on all four at once. A good lead magnet is the cleanest example: they learn something they want (high outcome), the output is guaranteed (high likelihood), results in seconds (no delay), a short form (no effort).

## The free-deliverable hierarchy

Two things decide what you lead with, and one constraint gates both.

**By value.** A free slice of the actual service is always worth far more than a sample, a teardown or a lead magnet. If the client will do a free deliverable, lead with that.

**By awareness.** Less aware, lower-intent prospects are more intrigued by information (a lead magnet, a Loom, an overview). More aware, higher-intent prospects respond to a free deliverable or a guarantee-based offer. Testing confirms which wins.

**The gate: what the client will agree to.** Not every client will work for free. Work backwards from their willingness. The ideal is the free deliverable. The real starting point is the best offer inside what they'll allow.

Present these four front-end directions on the onboarding call:

| Direction | Performance | Cost to the client |
|---|---|---|
| Do the deliverable up front, free | Best | Highest |
| Low-friction trial | Middle | Middle |
| Free value on the call itself (for example, a free valuation) | Middle | Low |
| Lead magnet or sales asset | Converts worst on the back end | Nothing |

## Match awareness and sophistication

### Awareness

Don't assume it. Test for it. On a first launch, build offer variations for every awareness level and let the market tell you. The CTA is where awareness shows most (see [copy.md](copy.md)).

Exception: a strong signal like hiring. Those prospects know they have the problem. Aim at the most aware and use a harder ask.

### Sophistication (Eugene Schwartz)

| Level | Market state | What wins |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | New market | Any clear claim |
| 2 | Competitors appear | Bigger claims |
| 3 | Claims are tired | A unique mechanism |
| 4 | Mechanisms are common | Better mechanisms, stronger guarantees |
| 5 | Maximum skepticism | Personal brand, evidence and a guarantee together |

Offers decay. Every offer slides over time as competitors copy it. Three free ad creatives used to work on their own. It had to become three free video ads. When a direct offer stops working, make it better.

### Demand capture vs generation

- **Capture:** they already look for it (bookkeeping, IT, recruiting). Lead with differentiation and upfront value, like an audit or competitive teardown.
- **Generation:** you create the demand. Lead with outcomes and the cost of doing nothing.
- **The two sales.** If you target people not already buying this kind of thing, the market is far bigger, but you sell the concept first and yourself second. Adjust the value prop to which group you're targeting.

## Guarantees are supplements, not the offer

Guarantees and risk reversals belong in copy, never as the whole pitch. They help overcome objections. Put them in parentheses or a P.S.

- **Gate on sophistication.** In sophisticated markets (e-commerce), a guarantee won't sway anyone alone. In less sophisticated markets (local service businesses), it helps more.
- **Anchor it to a known objection** from your research brief. Not a generic money-back reflex.
- **It rides along.** "We'll make you 3 free UGC ad creatives, and if they don't beat your current ads, you pay nothing." The offer is the creatives. The risk reversal is the tail.

Claims, Guarantees, Evidence (CGE): prospects ask in that order. What will I get? What if it doesn't work? Who else got it? No evidence means you need both a claim and a guarantee. Strong evidence lets you drop the guarantee. People weigh social proof more than risk reversals.

If the client does use a guarantee, it needs stipulations: things the client's customer must already have (state) or must do (action) to qualify. Good stipulations are just what's needed to get the result anyway.

## Lead with the slice, never the whole thing

You can't sell the dream in one email. One solution, to one prospect, for one specific pain. That is almost never the client's entire offer. It's one value prop.

- Every campaign is front-end-offer-first: give something valuable, free, that solves that one problem.
- The full mechanism is step two. It happens in the Loom, the asset or the call, never in the email.
- Which slice wins is a market decision. Test value props across personas and let the market pick.

## The angles

These are the offer types to put in your test matrix. Each has a fuller lesson. Pick by awareness and by what the client will allow.

| Angle | What it is | Best when |
|---|---|---|
| Direct offer | The email is the offer: one line on what they get, soft ask | The thing is desirable enough to stand alone, or the client already gives something free in their sales process |
| Service offering | "If we could connect you with 3-5 companies looking for {core_product_offering}..." | Any growth offer. Covers for a broad, messy list |
| ICP / introduction | "If we could introduce you to {ICP} looking for {their service}..." | Growth offers. Great for interest, weaker for show rate |
| Case study | Your result as the asset, or their own website case study as the personalization | Lead gen and growth offers. Medium awareness |
| Lead magnet | An interactive tool (calculator, audit, assessment) that creates a gap | Lower awareness. After the angle has already won |
| Loom | A short video as the low-friction thing you offer | Less aware prospects, or a client who won't give work away |
| Trojan horse | A ~10 word question you already know the answer to, offer comes in message two | New infrastructure or a brand new client, first two weeks |
| Partnership | Offer businesses that share the client's customers a new revenue stream | Saturated end market, or a small TAM resting between rounds |

### Notes on each

- **Direct.** If nothing is free, find the reason to pick this client over the next one: cheaper, faster, or a unique method. Don't ask people who aren't in market if they want to buy. Offer a preliminary step instead (for M&A, a market valuation, not "want to sell?").
- **Service offering and ICP.** The variables do the work. Large databases are only 60-70% accurate on what a company does, so per-lead enrichment carries the relevance. Always set a fallback value so no email goes out with a hole. Test these two against each other, don't run both in one campaign. For ICP, add a timeline line ("a few of them over the next two to four weeks") so calls don't book with the wrong expectation.
- **Case study.** Your own: result, time window, permission to send. Theirs: pull one client name off their site and offer more clients like that one. Never name a client or reuse one client's results without permission for that asset. No case studies yet? Lean on industry expertise and their lingo.
- **Lead magnet.** A tool beats a document. A dense PDF lets them do it themselves. The tool should turn on one input they can't fill in precisely, like their total addressable market. That gap is your front-end offer. Deliver it without a call ask. Expect low conversion and plan nudges (3-4 step subsequences, setter calls).
- **Loom.** The email sells the Loom, the Loom sells the call, the call sells the service. Record it on their website. Keep it out of the technical and keep the loop open. Most people who ask won't watch it, so follow up.
- **Trojan horse.** Reply rates run roughly 2x normal with a good question. Book rate is much lower. Move back to the full offer around week two or three. Detail in [infrastructure.md](infrastructure.md).
- **Partnership.** One partner can bring 10-15 sales instead of one. Pitch it as a revenue stream for them, not a sale.

## Write 3 value props and 3 front-end offers

### Before you write

Cross-check the research brief against what has already worked in similar markets. Which proven structures map here? Adapt, don't copy. Classify demand (capture or generation) and the sophistication level.

### 3 value props

Each one must:

- Target a **different validated pain** from the brief.
- Promise a **specific, measurable outcome** where you can.
- Be **1-2 lines**, sound like a human said it, no brochure language.
- Use risk reversal or urgency only if it genuinely strengthens it.

After each, add 1-2 sentences of reasoning with sources, and a reference note if a proven framework or past campaign informed it.

Generic tone examples (for format only, never copy):

- "10-20 qualified sellers for your real estate business every month"
- "Rank #1 for your keyword on Google in 90 days (or you don't pay)"

### 3 front-end offers (one per value prop)

A front-end offer is not the main service. It is a free or heavily discounted first step that:

- Gets a foot in the door and proves value fast.
- Removes risk and commitment.
- Is **specific and tangible**. "A free call" or "a consultation" doesn't count.
- Asks the prospect to **invest time**, so it leads to a call.
- Connects clearly to its value prop.

Shapes that work:

- "I can show you [3-4 things] in your [platform] that will immediately lift [metric]."
- "Let me do [one unit of work]. If it beats your current [metric], we talk."
- "I'll [deliver one specific asset]. If you like it, we discuss more."
- A custom build of their [50 best-fit leads], a free audit of their [current process], [1 free unit] of the service.

### Score it (target 5+/7)

1. Mirrors their self-identity, not their job title?
2. Names a real tension they feel but rarely hear said?
3. Anti-positions against what they already dismissed?
4. Aspiration is personal and specific, not a tagline?
5. Short enough to signal confidence?
6. CTA answerable in one word?
7. Variants spin words, not psychology?

Below 4, rework before showing anyone.

### The review gate

Present the 3 value props and 3 front-end offers, then stop and ask:

> "Would you like 3 new value propositions, or are you happy with these and ready to move to email copy?"

- **New ones:** write 3 entirely new ones. Different pains, outcomes and framing. Don't recycle language. Ask the gate again after every round.
- **Happy:** confirm the final set and hand off to [copy.md](copy.md).

The offer is a human gate. A bot never approves its own offer. The student (or their client) signs off.

## Common mistakes

- Pitching the service instead of the offer.
- Building from an assumed pain, not a validated one.
- Selling the whole system in one email.
- Making the guarantee the whole pitch.
- A generic money-back reversal not tied to a real objection.
- Going direct with an offer that isn't desirable on its own.
- Running a free offer long after it has fatigued.
- Writing email copy here. Stop at the offer.
